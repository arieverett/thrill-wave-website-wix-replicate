## What this is and who it's for

This brief is everything needed to build the new thrillwave.com: one page, no menu. Build it from this document, launch it once, then leave the copy alone until mid-January.

**Who reads the site.** Someone who was referred to us and is checking that we're real before they call. Usually a founder, an executive director, a program director with a budget, or a communications lead who is tired of safe, forgettable video. They are not filmmakers. Write so a fifth-grader could follow it.

**What the site has to do.** Convince that person in one scroll. It should also put off people shopping for the cheapest videographer, without insulting the smaller clients who find us through Thumbtack (our smallest work there is a shoot day with the footage handed over).

**What the site does not have to do.** Bring in leads on its own. Chris's daily lead tool and direct outreach do that.

**The position, in Chris's words.** The site must say the same thing the partners say out loud.

- Short version: "We make films about hard subjects and hard places for organizations that can't afford to get it wrong."
- Long version: "We run Thrill Wave, a production company in Phoenix. We make films for organizations where the project has to be right the first time. Most of our work is the kind of project people get nervous about: controversial, emotionally heavy, morally complicated, or logistically risky, and usually a bigger swing than the brand has taken before. We do the research that makes them land instead of blow up, and we'll shoot wherever the story is, including places most crews won't go. Long form, because that's what builds trust. Most crews make the easy video. We get hired when the easy video isn't worth making."

**The two kinds of hard.** Hard subjects: controversial, emotionally heavy, morally complicated, a bigger swing than the client has taken before. Hard places: remote, uncontrolled, high-risk, unwelcoming to a camera. A project only needs one of these to be ours. A senator in a quiet office is a hard subject in an easy place. A guide three days into the backcountry is an easy subject in a hard place: two or three people, what they can carry, and no other crew willing to bid. Hard subjects are listed before hard places because that is why our biggest clients hire us. ITCA and public-health bodies pay for sensitive subjects handled well; hard places mostly win smaller jobs.

**Two things that run through everything.** We prefer long form, because time spent is what builds trust. And we treat brand risk as something to manage, not avoid; that is what lets a nervous client say yes. Behind both is research. On the page we call it homework, never technology.

## Writing rules

For anyone who changes a word on the page, now or later.

- Short sentences. Numbers instead of adjectives. Fifth-grade reading level. No exclamation points. No intensifiers like "period," "every time," or "literally." No bold in body text except the block labels.
- Never use these words: elite, world-class, premier, award-winning, creative partner, stories with purpose, bring your vision to life, cinematic, passionate, bespoke, full-service, end-to-end, solutions, content creation, brain rot, boring.
- Never mention camera or lens brands and models (drones and cable cam are fine as services), the SITREP by name (our pre-production research process; it belongs in proposals), music videos as a category, or anything saying short-form video is bad.
- Call research "homework." Never say AI, OSINT, intelligence, data, or synthesis on the page; those words go in proposals. The page says real things are worth more than generated things, so a paragraph about AI would read as a contradiction.
- Describe drone work by where we fly, never by how close. Never "sketchy," "near people," or "near critical infrastructure," even when true. Our insurance carrier and the FAA read websites.
- Describe what we do. Never describe what other crews do badly. "Boring" was cut for sounding petty; apply that test to every line.
- Name projects, clients, and numbers only in the proof line and the Selected work captions. The headline and the pitch never name a specific place.
- The two edgy lines ("die in the PR meeting," "legal is nervous about") are deliberate. Soften them in a proposal if a client balks, never on the site.

## Appendix: the full manifesto (not on the page)

The seventeen-line version, kept here as source material for longer pieces, ordered boldest first to match block 6. The page uses the nine-line version in block 6.

1. Trying to offend no one is how you convince no one.
2. The safe idea costs the same to make, and nobody remembers it. A cheap video is worse than none; it tells people what you think of them.
3. Brand risk is something we manage, not something we avoid. If the easy version won't work, we say so, even when it's your plan.
4. Conflict is what makes a story a story. A film with no tension is a brochure, and the best stories have conflicted people in them.
5. People decide with their gut and explain with facts. Make the film for the gut.
6. A film is a demand for someone's time. It has to be worth more than whatever else they could do with it.
7. Long form builds trust. Short form builds reach. Give a story the time it deserves, and people will give it theirs.
8. Vulnerability is the fastest way to be believed. People trust what costs something to say.
9. Safe is for the crew, not the idea. Careful on the ground is what makes the risk on screen possible.
10. A crew should be small enough to get in, quiet enough that people forget it, and quick enough to move when the story does.
11. Some stories are hard to reach. Some are hard to tell. We're built for both, together or apart.
12. When anything can be generated, the real thing is worth more. We film real people in real places.
13. Trust is scarce now. People don't hand it out, and they're right not to.
14. People are drowning in content. Most of it is forgettable, a lot of it is fake, and all of it is fighting for the same two seconds.
15. Plans fail on location. Good crews expect that and pack for it.
16. Homework is most of the job. We know the subject, the people, and the place before the camera comes out.
17. We finish. On time, on budget, no surprises.
