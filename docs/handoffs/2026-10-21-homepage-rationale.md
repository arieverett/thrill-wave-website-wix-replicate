The rest of the site stays, with its menu; "one page, no menu" applies to the homepage's own structure (nine blocks, no in-page navigation), not to the site. ## 1. What the site is for

The site has one job: when someone is referred to Thrill Wave and opens the page to check we're real, it convinces them in one scroll. It does not generate leads. Chris's daily lead tool and direct outreach do that. Every design and copy choice follows from that single job.

**The buyer.** The person inside an organization who can approve risk and wants to: a founder, an executive director, a program director with budget, a communications lead who is tired of safe, forgettable video. Not filmmakers. Not marketing managers who need three approvals. The copy is written at a fifth-grade reading level because this person is smart but does not speak production.

**The buyer's internal sentence.** "This project has to be right the first time, and I can't risk an unproven crew." That sentence is why the position works: Thrill Wave is the safe choice for the risky project. The edge (we'll take on what others won't) gets attention. The competence (we make it hold up) gets the signature. Edge without competence reads as reckless, and the nervous PR director wins the internal argument every time.

**Three readers who aren't buyers but will read the page.**

- A PR or legal person deciding whether Thrill Wave is reckless. The page has to give them a sentence they can forward to their boss as a reason to say yes. That sentence is "Brand risk is something we manage, not something we avoid."
- An insurance underwriter. Underwriters read websites. That is why drone work is described by where we fly, never by how close.
- Anyone checking a drone claim. Same rule. The FAA reads websites too.

**The Thumbtack reality.** Smaller clients find Thrill Wave through Thumbtack. The smallest work comes from there: a shoot day with the footage handed over. The page must not insult those clients. "A lazy video is worse than none" indicts the $500 highlight reel, not them, and the Production block says plainly that teams with their own editors can book the day and take the raws. Shoot-only is a product, not a compromise.

**What the page must never do.** Repel the smallest client to impress the biggest one. Lead with risk. Read as polarizing for its own sake. The team removed "boring" from an earlier draft because it read as petty; that test applies to every line.

## 2. How the position was reached

The position is: **We make films about hard subjects and hard places for organizations that can't afford to get it wrong.** It took four tries in October to get there, and the code should understand why the earlier versions lost, so it doesn't drift back toward them.

**The logic underneath (from Ries and Trout's *Positioning*, which Chris is reading).** You cannot win a head-on fight against agencies with more money and more talent, so don't claim creative superiority. Find the hole in the market nobody occupies. Then reposition the competition so their strength looks like a weakness. Thrill Wave's hole is "difficult." The repositioning is "most crews make the easy video."

**The four versions.**

1. Oct 9: "films that make you feel." Rejected by the team itself on Oct 20 as generic; every production company says it.
2. Oct 14: five pages, three services, the SITREP research process folded into high-ticket packages instead of being a public tool. Still holds, except the page count.
3. Oct 20: "logistical and emotional difficulty" and "risk tolerance over creative superiority," with a single-page site, a manifesto, and a reel. The right idea, phrased for the team rather than for the buyer.
4. Oct 21: the current line, phrased for the buyer, with the two pillars made independent.

**Why this one holds.** Every paying client in 2026 fits it: the ITCA voter-rights series (sensitive subject, tribal chairmen and the Governor), the tribal vaccine spots (skeptical audience), the Relentless Beats festivals (chaos), EDP's working site. It describes a buying moment, not a talent claim: "we get hired when the easy video isn't worth making." And it is a claim about what the crew will take on, which competitors can't copy by hiring a better editor.

**Thrill Wave does not own this position yet.** It has a small book of business that fits it and clients who keep hiring it for that kind of work. The site is the first public commitment to the position, which is why the declaration (what we do, for whom) and the manifesto carry the weight. The reel and the stills support what the words say and convey style and ability; they do not have to prove difficulty frame by frame. Insinuation is fine. Not every image needs a visibly hard situation.

**The two pillars.** Hard to say out loud (controversial, emotionally heavy, morally complicated, a bigger swing than the client has taken before) and hard to get to (remote, uncontrolled, high-risk, unwelcoming to a camera). They are independent. A senator in a quiet office is a hard subject in an easy place. A guide three days into the backcountry is an easy subject in a hard place: two or three people, what they can carry, and no other crew willing to bid. Either one qualifies a project. Subjects lead on the page because that is why the biggest clients hire Thrill Wave; hard places mostly win smaller jobs.

**Brand risk, handled correctly.** The team is tired of pitching good ideas that die in a client's PR meeting as "too risky." The temptation is to position as the crew that doesn't care about brand risk. That loses. The position is that brand risk gets managed, not avoided: context, consent, review, and homework before the pitch. The ITCA series had editorial guardrails around depicting arrestees and protecting victims. That record is what makes the edge sellable.

**Long form as the trust mechanism.** The team prefers long form because time spent is what builds trust in whoever publishes the piece. The page says so without trashing short form, because most buyers need cutdowns and the portfolio contains short work (the vaccine spots, a streaming ad). The rule: make the long version first, cut the short ones from it. "Short form is brain rot" stays in the room and out of the copy.

**Research is the mechanism under both pillars.** Thrill Wave's deep-research and open-source-intelligence habit is how the controversial piece holds up and how a small crew gets into a hard place knowing what it's walking into. On the page this is called homework, never AI, OSINT, data, or synthesis. Those words belong in proposals, where method is scored. The reconciling line for any room: machines read, people film.

**What was deliberately not made the identity.** Getting straight answers out of officials is a real skill (the voter series needed it) but "interrogation specialist" is not a thing anyone hires. It lives inside "hard to get right" and homework. A signature camera look (35mm, vintage bodies) is a creative choice, not a differentiator, and it's money the company doesn't have.

**The name is settled.** Thrill Wave stays. Internally it names the mechanism: the wave of emotion in the viewer that moves them and drives action. Good work is thrilling to watch. Do not read "thrill" as extreme sports or adrenaline imagery, do not build visuals around that reading, and do not propose a rename.

## 4. Voice rules and the failure each one prevents

The register does the work adjectives usually do. Firms that are elite never say so; the sentence structure says it for them. These rules apply to any text that reaches the page, including alt text, button labels, and error messages.

| Rule | What it prevents |
| --- | --- |
| Short declaratives. Numbers instead of adjectives | Sounding like every agency site ("stories with purpose," "bring your vision to life") |
| Fifth-grade reading level; split any sentence that needs reading twice | Losing a smart buyer who doesn't speak production, on a phone, in one scroll |
| No exclamation points, nothing underlined, no bold in body copy except block labels | Hype register; the page should state, never insist |
| No intensifiers: period, every time, literally, categorically, you name it | Same; each one weakens the claim it decorates |
| Banned words: elite, world-class, premier, award-winning, creative partner, stories with purpose, bring your vision to life, cinematic, passionate, bespoke, full-service, end-to-end, solutions, content creation, brain rot, boring | The first eleven are what competitors say; the last two are what the team says in the room and must not say in public |
| No camera or lens brands or models; drones and cable cam are fine as services | Gear is a look, not a reason to hire |
| SITREP never named on the page | A method doesn't sell on a landing page; it's scored in proposals |
| Research is "homework," never AI, OSINT, intelligence, data, synthesis | Direct contradiction with "the real thing is worth more than the generated thing" |
| Drone work by where, never by how close; never sketchy, near people, near critical infrastructure | Liability; insurance and regulators read websites |
| Describe what Thrill Wave does; never what other crews do badly | Reads petty; "boring" was cut for exactly this |
| Specifics (projects, clients, numbers, places) only in the proof line and the Selected work captions | Everywhere else stays general so the firm reads wide, not niche |
| Never invent detail for texture | The "4am" lesson; one invented fact discredits the true ones |
| Never write a testimonial; ask the client one question and quote the answer | Credibility, and it's the only block a buyer fact-checks |
| Long form is the preference; short form is never called bad | Buyers need cutdowns; the portfolio has short pieces |
| The edge lines ("die in the PR meeting," "legal is nervous about," "offend no one," "conflicted people") stay on the site; they get softened in proposals to government clients, never on the page | The site declares the position; a proposal is scored by a committee |
| The headline is the only line allowed to be clever | More than one and the page is performing instead of telling |

## 5. Page architecture and build guidance

One page, no menu, nine blocks in a fixed order. The order is an argument: declare, prove, name the two kinds of hard, list what we do, show the work, state what we believe, let clients vouch, ask for contact. Reordering blocks breaks the argument.

**What each block has to do.**

1. **Hero.** Declare the position in two lines. Plain headline, the eight-second line as subhead. No client logos under it; logos are what every agency does and they say nothing about difficulty.
2. **Proof line.** Give the reader a reason to keep scrolling: subjects, places, one big number. Lands before the services because proof is why they stay.
3. **Two kinds of hard.** Name the pillars, state that one is enough, give each its proof. Side by side on desktop, stacked on mobile, with the "one of these is enough" line above both.
4. **What we do.** Three services plus the long-form stance. Story leads because homework is the mechanism under everything else. Rentals and photography live inside Production.
5. **Selected work.** Five to seven stills with captions. The captions carry all the specifics on the page. No category labels. The declaration and the manifesto do the heavy lifting; the stills support them by conveying style and ability. Suggest difficulty where the frame allows (the remote location, the stage build, the interview where someone broke, the crowd), but not every image needs a visibly hard situation.
6. **What we believe.** Nine lines, boldest first, most agreeable last, ending on the delivery promise. Spaced, not bulleted. Not labeled "Manifesto."
7. **What clients say.** Three quotes with name, title, organization. Hidden entirely if fewer than two are in hand.
8. **Contact.** One sentence, email, phone, city, founding year. Form: name, email, one open text box.
9. **Browser title and search description.** "Thrill Wave | Films about hard subjects and hard places" and the eight-second line verbatim. This is what a referral sees in a text-message preview before the page loads, so it is the first copy most readers encounter.

Optional: a strip of behind-the-scenes stills shot on Portra film, captions of five words or fewer, no copy. Launch without it if not ready.

**Rules for the code.**

- Copy comes from the brief's quote boxes, verbatim. Do not paraphrase, tighten, or "improve" a line. Every word was argued over; a rewrite reintroduces the versions in Section 3.
- Bracketed text in the brief is instruction, never copy.
- Block labels (What we do, Selected work, What we believe) may appear as small section headings. Do not add headings, taglines, or microcopy the brief doesn't contain.
- Hide, don't placeholder. A block missing its asset (quotes, reel, BTS) is removed from the render, not shown with lorem ipsum or "coming soon."
- Mobile first. The referral reads this on a phone between meetings. Two-column blocks stack; the stills grid becomes a single column; nothing requires hover.
- The reel, if present, autoplays muted with captions available, and the page must read fully with the reel slot empty.
- Alt text for stills reuses the caption; alt text for the reel is the eight-second line.
- Typography follows the register: no all-caps shouting, no underlines, no exclamation points anywhere, including buttons. Curly apostrophes and quotes in rendered text.
- No fonts or colors were specified in this process. Do not invent brand flourishes to fill that gap; the Oct 20 direction was ultra-minimalist, work speaks, minimal reader effort. When in doubt, remove.
- Performance over effects. A page that loads instantly on cellular in a parking lot beats one with transitions.
- Nothing on the page links to a research tool, a workbench, or anything called SITREP. There is no password gate and no gated content.
- No budget dropdown, no multi-step form, no chatbot.

**After launch.** Freeze until mid-January. Allowed changes: add a client quote, add a Selected work caption, fix a factual error. Everything else waits. Three signals tell us it worked: new leads name the site when asked how they found us; the CSTE proposal scores well on experience; new inquiries describe their projects as sensitive, complicated, or tricky. If none of those move, the problem is lead flow, not the website, and the fix is outreach, not a rewrite.
