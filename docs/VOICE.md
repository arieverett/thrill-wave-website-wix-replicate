# Thrill Wave voice guide

How every page, post and line on thrillwave.com (and terminal.thrillwave.com) should sound. When in doubt, read sandwich.co out loud, then do the opposite of lookstudios.co.

## Who we are, in one breath

Thrill Wave is a video production company in Phoenix. We care about story, about the tech and engineering behind the image, about art, and most of all about people. We see storytelling as a discipline: a way to connect people, teach, make hard things simpler to understand, make things better, reflect and move forward.

People come to us for our expertise, so we lead. We do the research, we have a point of view, and we guide clients to the story worth telling.

## What we believe (manifesto draft v0.2, Chris, Oct 9, 2026)

The manifesto is a draft the founders are still working through. The full text is on the About page ("What we believe."); this is the short version every line of copy should agree with.

**We sell a way of working, not a crew.** The chain: nothing changes anyone unless they feel something real; nothing real comes out unless someone goes and gets it; getting it is the hard part (the research, the hard questions, the patience to sit with someone until they say the true thing, and the nerve to keep it in the cut). That's what we do. The camera comes last. Every page should make the hard part the thing we're known for: other shops promise impact and emotion, and nobody sells the part where someone has to go and get the truth out of real people.

- **There's no such thing as an audience, only people.** People take in a story in remarkably similar ways. Great stories work because they speak to the human drive for story and feeling: grief, fear, love, loyalty, ambition. Viewers don't need to have lived what's on screen to feel it; it has to be true about being human. So we don't make films for demographics or target segments, and copy shouldn't promise to "reach your audience" or match a viewer's own experience.
- **No actors (hard rule).** Thrill Wave is a nonfiction house: real people telling real stories in their real lives. We don't direct performances and we don't make scripted drama. The drama is essential, but it comes from what actually happened to someone, never from casting. The manifesto line: "We don't direct actors. We get real people to say true things."
- **We find the feeling, we don't add it (hard rule).** Keep the nevers next to any claim about emotion, so it can't read as tear-jerking: no narration, no actors, no stock, nothing telling the viewer what to feel. The chain says why we work this way; the nevers prove it.
- **"Blood, sweat and tears built this boring world."** Use the line word for word. Every organization assumes its story is dull; nobody's is, they just haven't let anyone look. Use this framing on Who we serve and anywhere copy speaks to businesses, professional services, builders and makers. Its proof is the boring-company films: Rumco, Citgo and Brundage Bone, Payroc (not ITCA).
- **Timeless art is the safe bet.** The safe-looking version (follow the trend, copy a peer, aim at a demographic) is the risky one: it dates fast and only works on the segment it was aimed at. A film built on human truth keeps working for anyone, for years. That makes timeless art the safe, responsible and smart way to spend money on a film. Say it plainly and with conviction.
- **SITREP is step one.** You can't ask the hard question without knowing the world, so SITREP is the start of going and getting the true thing. Keep describing it as research and a point of view, not a standalone product.
- **There's no neutral film.** Every cut is a choice about what stays and what goes. We take a side and say whose. We don't lecture, trick anyone or claim what we can't show, and the people in the frame should recognize themselves.
- **The Thrill Wave treatment.** Clients don't hire a few people with cameras; they get a way of working. Every project has a safe version and a good version, and we push for the good one and say why. An aftermovie gets the same care as a docuseries, because to the person it's for, it matters just as much. We don't put our name on anything we don't mean.
- **The proof, claim by claim.** For the trust claim: Dear Mom and the vaccine films (ITCA WIC and TEC). For "the method is an approach, not a genre", the pair: *Your Voice, Your Power* (ITCA Native Vote) was briefed as an educational series on how government works; we argued that nobody watches homework, and each of the ten episodes follows a person instead of a civics lesson. The *Decadence* aftermovies (Relentless Beats) are cut for the fans who build their year around two nights at Phoenix Raceway, not for a demographic. For the boring-world claim: Rumco, Citgo and Brundage Bone, Payroc.
- **Careful with facts.** Until ITCA okays it, don't name anyone in *Your Voice, Your Power* or describe individual episodes, and claim nothing about its viewers or results (it hasn't aired). Say Relentless Beats has trusted us "for years". The rise in ITCA WIC participation after Dear Mom is a public USDA number, not a result ITCA has attributed to the film: present it as a public figure from around that time, never as what the film did. Decadence 2025 attendance (about 50,000) is EDM Maniac's estimate; credit it that way.
- **The name.** A thrill wave is what goes through a person when something true hits them; "thrill" comes from an old word meaning to pierce. One line on About is plenty. Don't build a section or a lecture around it.

## SITREP

SITREP is our research. Before we film, we dig deep into the client's craft, their peers, their industry and their sector. Think of it as a third-party R&D department that cares deeply about the story, lines up with the client's goals and helps direct it. It's how we lead.

Describe it in plain words. It's research and a point of view, not a product pitch, not a "proprietary software platform", not "data-driven insights". The software we built for it can come up briefly; the research and the point of view are the story.

## Where stories live

Almost every great story, from Shakespeare to the best documentaries, plays out against one of three backdrops: athletics, the arts or academics (science, medicine, engineering, research, law and governance). Our industries are grouped that way (the site calls them "Fields"; the address stays /industries for search), and copy about them can lean on it: the setting changes, but the human story underneath doesn't.

## How we report

We work the way good documentary reporting does: we report first, check our sources, follow the facts, let people speak for themselves and never stage what should be felt. Use that approach and terminology (report, sources, facts, on the record, in their own words), but never name it: don't write "journalism", "journalist" or "reporter".

## How it sounds

- **Like a conversation.** Every page reads like one of us walking someone through it over coffee: a short, clear elevator pitch that flows from the top of the page to the bottom and ends with an invitation to write to us.
- **Warm, plain and confident, and willing to take a side.** Short, precise sentences that explain one thing each, like sandwich.co. Say the bold thing plainly and stop: no hype, no exclamation points. A little humor is welcome when it's natural.
- **One idea, one home.** Say each manifesto idea once, in the place it belongs, and let every other page say it in its own words. The manifesto lines themselves ("The camera comes last", "Blood, sweat and tears built this boring world", "We don't direct actors...") live on About; "Blood, sweat and tears" may also open Who we serve. Don't paste them into every page, and don't drop in examples a founder used to make a point in conversation (Dune, for instance). Shorthand like "the true thing" belongs in the manifesto, not repeated through page copy.
- **Bold and specific, never vague.** Make bold claims about our method, our approach and our worldview, and back each one with something concrete: how people actually behave (in front of a camera, in front of a statistic, in front of a lecture), a hard part of the job most crews skip, or a real film of ours. Lines that sound deep but say nothing ("people are moved by someone they recognize", "the crowd recognizes itself", "people don't feel for crowds") don't make the cut. Don't build copy around making a film "for one specific person"; the point is the truth that's shared by everyone. Any research we cite must be checked against the original source.
- **Full sentences only.** No fragments ("Real people. Real stakes."), no slogan stacks, no one-word labels standing in for a thought. Headlines can be short, but body copy is always complete sentences.
- **Explicit, never left to chance.** Say what the next thing on the page is. When a paragraph introduces a list, grid, films or cards, end it with a sentence that leads into them, finishing with a colon. Vary it: "Here are a few of the films we've made for them:", "This is what that looks like in practice:", "Here's who we work with most:".
- **Top down, left to right, toward a message.** Each section hands off to the next. The page ends by asking the reader to reach out.
- **Humble expertise.** Show it through specifics (what we noticed, how we did it, what gear, what changed) rather than claims. "Your friendly neighborhood filmmakers" is the spirit: approachable, quietly very good.

## Words (hard rules, Oct 9, 2026)

These apply everywhere: pages, content JSON, blocks, the Tuesday Intel posts and llms.txt. Use them consistently and never explain them on the site; let readers notice.

- **"Video production company"** is the category phrase, because it's what people search. It stays in titles, meta descriptions, the Google Business Profile and the first line of the homepage, and that's the only place "video" describes our own work. (Talking about other people's videos, or the industry in general, is fine.)
- **Everything we author is a "film":** the docuseries, the aftermovie, the team film, the explainer. Derivatives are **"cuts"**: vertical cuts, 30-second cuts, social cuts. Never stretch "film" over a 9:16 clip.
- **We're "filmmakers" or "our crew"**, never "videographers".
- **Avoid "audience"** (and "target audience", "segments", "demographics", "personas") except when the copy is arguing against the idea. Say "people" (or "one person" where it reads naturally, without building the copy around making a film for one specific person).
- No "content", no "campaign", no "agency" for us, no AI in our process, no headcount, no em dashes, no exclamation points.

**Delivery rule:** every film we deliver ends on a card that reads "A Thrill Wave film". It's the "we put our name on it" beat made literal. The site doesn't show it; it's a rule for the films themselves.

## Never

- Never mention how many of us there are, call us friends, or call us small, tiny, lean or scrappy. Use "we", "our founders", "our crew", "our team".
- Never call Thrill Wave an agency (marketing, ad or creative). It's a production company. Agencies can be clients.
- Never actors, casting or scripted drama in our own work. We make nonfiction with real people.
- No agency or big-business speak, buzzwords or jargon: no "leverage", "synergy", "solutions", "drive results", "elevate", "unlock", "full-service", "end-to-end", "best-in-class", "ROI-driven", "content", "campaign", "deliverables", "assets", "stakeholders", "brand storytelling", "data-driven", "proprietary".
- No selling, pitching or market talk. No urgency, no "get a quote today", no claims we can't show.
- Nothing that echoes lookstudios.co's copy or layout: no "what makes it tricky" cards, no catalog of formats with lengths and "from $" prices, no walls of FAQs, no filler stats from third parties. Some familiar formats are fine in our own voice (Oct 3, 2026): the "what we hear" cards on Services (a client's words as the heading, our answer in full sentences, a "What helps:" line), the five promises and our own numbers on Services, and the four numbered points on industry and customer pages ("Who we work with / How we help / What we make / Where it plays", each a full sentence, with a lead-in).
- No em dashes or spaced dashes (use a comma, colon or new sentence). No exclamation-point hype.
- Never mention AI in our own process.

## Words we like

Films, cuts, filmmakers, stories, people, person, craft, research, the true thing, the edit, the light, set, shoot, crew, care, honest, clear, simple, together.

## Quick test before publishing

1. Read it out loud. Does it sound like a person talking?
2. Is every sentence a full sentence?
3. Does every paragraph that comes before a list or grid tell the reader what's coming, ending in a colon?
4. Would someone who reached the bottom of the page know exactly what to do next?
5. Could this line appear on any other production company's site? If so, make it more specific or cut it.
