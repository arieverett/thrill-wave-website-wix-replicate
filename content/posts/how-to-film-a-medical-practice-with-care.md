Most people meet a new doctor at a bad moment: they're worried, a little lost and short on time. A good film can meet them earlier, when they're calm enough to listen. It can explain a condition in plain words, show them the room they'll walk into and let them hear from someone who's been where they are.

That's a different job from getting noticed. A film for a medical practice has to be clear, honest and kind before it's anything else. Here's what we've learned about the three films that do that best, and how to make each one well:

1. **The explainer:** a clinician walks through a condition, a treatment or a procedure in language a patient can follow.
2. **The walkthrough:** a calm look at the office and the people in it, so the first visit feels familiar.
3. **The patient story:** someone who's been through it tells it in their own words.

## The explainer: make hard things simple

Clinicians know their field so well that they forget what it sounds like from the outside. Our job is to find the plain version. Before we film, we sit down with the doctor and ask the questions patients actually ask, in the words patients actually use. Then we let the doctor answer those, not a script.

Keep each explainer to one question. A patient who wants to know what recovery looks like doesn't need the history of the procedure. A clear diagram or a simple model on the desk often teaches more than a minute of talking, so we plan those shots in advance. ## The walkthrough: show the room before the visit

Plenty of people feel nervous about a new practice, and a lot of that comes from not knowing what happens next. Where do I park? Who do I talk to first? What does the exam room look like? A short walkthrough answers those quietly.

We film it the way a patient would experience it: the front door, the front desk, the waiting room, the room where care happens. We keep the people natural. A real hello from the person at the desk does more than any line we could write. And we film on a day, or at an hour, when no patients are in the frame.

## The patient story: consent first, then the camera

Nothing helps a worried person more than hearing from someone who's been there. That also makes these the films that need the most care. A patient's health is their own story, and they should know exactly where the film will be seen before they agree to tell it.

Here's what we sort out before anyone rolls:

| Before the shoot | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Written consent, with every place the film will play | Patients should know where their story ends up. |
| A clear line on what we won't show | Charts, screens and other patients stay out of frame. |
| The clinician's review of any medical claim | The facts have to be right, in the patient's words or the doctor's. |
| A quiet, private place to talk | People speak more honestly when they feel safe. |

On the day, we let the patient talk at their own pace and in their own order. We don't feed lines. A rehearsed story sounds rehearsed, and patients can tell.

## If you run a practice

If you're thinking about films for your practice, start with the questions your patients ask most. The best medical films answer them clearly and kindly, and the trust follows.
