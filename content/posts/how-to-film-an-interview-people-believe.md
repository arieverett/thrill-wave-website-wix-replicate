People trust people. A good interview puts a real face and a real voice in front of an audience, and when it works, the viewer feels like they've met someone. When it doesn't, it sounds like a script read by a nervous person under hot lights, and everyone can tell.

The difference is almost never the camera. It's the choices made before, during and after the shoot. Here's what makes an interview believable, step by step:

1. **The right person:** someone who knows the story firsthand, not just the person with the best title.
2. **Real questions:** questions that lead to stories, not talking points.
3. **A comfortable room:** clean sound, soft light and a crew that knows when to step back.
4. **An honest edit:** one that keeps people in their own words.

## Pick the person, not the title

The first question is who should be on camera. It's tempting to pick the most senior person in the building. Often the better choice is the nurse who was in the room, the engineer who solved the problem or the customer who lived through it. People believe whoever was closest to the story.

Before a shoot, we do our research (we call it a [SITREP](/sitrep)) and talk to a few people informally. The best interviewee is usually the one who tells a specific story without being asked to, with names, places and details.

## Ask for stories, not answers

A question like "What makes your company great?" gets a brochure back. A question like "Tell me about the day you knew this was working" gets a story. We write questions that ask for moments, and we ask people to answer in full sentences so their answers stand on their own in the edit.

Then we listen. The best line of the day usually comes after the planned questions run out, when someone relaxes and says what they really think.

## Get the sound right, then the light

Viewers forgive a soft picture long before they forgive bad audio. Here's the order we set up an interview in, and why:

| Step | What we do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Sound | A lav mic and a boom, in the quietest room we can find | Clear sound is what keeps people watching. |
| Light | One soft key light and a little fill, nothing harsh | Gentle light looks natural and keeps people at ease. |
| Frame | The eye line just off camera, at eye height | It reads as a conversation, not a deposition. |
| Room | Fewer people on set and phones away | People open up when it doesn't feel like a stage. |

## Edit for honesty

In the edit, the goal is to keep people sounding like themselves. We cut for clarity, not to change what someone meant. Sometimes that means leaving in a pause or a laugh, because that's what makes it real.

## When to film one

An interview is the right film when the story belongs to a person: a patient, a founder, a teacher, a customer, a community. If you have someone whose story deserves to be heard in their own words, [message us](/contact) and we'll help you tell it well.
